## Abstract

Articles in Models and Words are about understanding social, biological, medical, sporting and technological systems, about the models we build of them, and about how those models relate to the words we use to describe them. We are looking for three types of contributions:

1. Applications which combine models and words to produce insight for stakeholders.
2. Methodological and theoretical contributions where models and words are combined.
3. Description of Agentic AI solutions, where human decision-making is placed in the centre.

Models and Words is itself an AI agent. Submissions are written together with an agent, reviews will be semi-automated and the repository itself can provide help and support to application builders and researchers.

## Introduction

New models change how we see the world. Kermack and McKendrick's model of epidemics showed that an outbreak can end before everyone has been infected [@kermack1927]. Turing proposed that two chemicals reacting and diffusing could produce the spots and stripes on animals [@turing1952], and Vicsek and Couzin showed that animals following simple rules about their neighbours' positions and directions produce the ordered motion of flocks and schools [@vicsek1995; @couzin2002]. In social science, Schelling showed that people with only mild preferences about their neighbours can produce strongly segregated cities [@schelling1971], an idea that grew into agent-based simulations of whole economies [@farmer2009; @axtell2025]. In sport, models of which space a football team controls [@spearman2018] and of the expected value of a basketball possession [@cervone2016] changed how games are analysed. @gyllingberg2023 argued that this art of formulating new models has (until recently) been neglected in favour of analysing the ones already written down. With agentic AI now able to systematically tick off many of the checklist of properties existing models have, @kasirzadeh2026 is among several researchers arguing for a greater focus on opening up new mathematical approaches. Coding has also changed. By the end of 2024, an estimated 29% of the Python functions contributed to open-source projects in the United States were generated by AI [@daniotti2025].  Programmers who used AI more often tried new libraries and new combinations of them, which the authors read as a move into areas of programming new to them 

Possibly an even more profound change brought about by Agentic AI lies in how it changes the relationship between words and models. Until recently, a model reached the people who used it through a data scientist, who knew which model answered which question, what it assumed and when it should not be trusted, and who explained its output in the language of the person deciding. Language models can now do part of that work themselves. A conversational analyst can choose which model answers a question and explain what the answer means [@sumpter2026earpiece], and a statistical model like logistic regression can be described in words, explaining its assumptions and intended use [@rahimian2025]. What was the data scientist's judgement about how and when to apply a model now has to be written down, so that an agent can follow it.

Model and Words publishes three types of article. The first type of article applies models and words to give people insight they act on. In the past year, applications that join language and statistical models have started to appear. For example, multi-agent systems have provided suggestions of how existing drugs might be used for new treatments [@gottweis2026,@ghareeb2026]  and studies have shown how research tasks can be carried out by agents throughout biomedicine [@huang2026]. Social systems have their own examples. An AI mediator that wrote group statements from participants' own opinions helped people on opposing sides of political issues find common ground, and its statements were preferred to those of human mediators [@tessler2024]. Personalised, evidence-based conversations with a language model reduced belief in conspiracy theories by about a fifth, and the effect lasted at least two months [@costello2024]. A language model fine-tuned on more than 160 psychology experiments predicted the choices of new participants better than existing cognitive models [@binz2025]. 


The second kind of article is methodological or theoretical, focusing in particular on relating models and words. Broadly speaking, there are at least three ways of getting from a model to words: build a model that can be read directly [@rudin2019], narrate the output of an explanation method afterwards [@zytek2024], or reverse-engineer the computations inside a neural network into human concepts [@bereska2024]. We welcome all three approaches. The first article in this report series gives a straightforward example of the first: showing how to turn a statistical model of the data into a rule-based description, deciding what is said and in what order, and a language model then writes only that description out as fluent text [@caut2025]. 

Language models are also being used to generate scientific hypotheses from data [@zhou2024]. @messeri2024 warn that tools of this kind can give scientists an illusion of understanding, so that science "produces more but understands less". Methods for checking what a text says against the model it describes are needed as much as methods for producing the text. Similarly, @rahimian2025 turned the coefficients of an expected goals model into sentences, which a language model then wrote up as a description of each shot. In both, the link between model and words is a set of written rules that a reader can inspect and change.

The third kind of article describes agentic AI systems built around a person's decision. @schluntz2024 distinguish workflows, in which language models and tools follow predefined code paths, from agents, in which the model directs its own process and use of tools. @shneiderman2020 argued that high automation and high human control can be designed together. The evidence on whether a human in the loop makes a difference is mixed. @bansal2021 found that AI explanations increased the chance that people accepted the AI's recommendation, whether it was right or wrong. In a randomised study of clinicians, a systematically biased model lowered diagnostic accuracy, and explanations did not help them recognise the bias [@jabbour2023]. In the United Kingdom, the Home Office trialled language-model tools that summarise asylum interviews and answer questions on country policy; the summaries saved caseworkers time, but some were removed as inaccurate or incomplete, and the government states that the tools do not replace any part of the decision [@homeoffice2025]. A legal Opinion later found that this use was at significant risk of being unlawful, in part because claimants were not told [@doughtystreet2026]. How the system is built decides what the person deciding actually sees.

Language models already take part in writing and reviewing science. An estimated 6.5 to 16.9 percent of peer-review text at four AI conferences in 2023 and 2024 was substantially modified by a language model [@liang2024monitoring], and the share of model-modified text in published papers has risen steadily, most in computer science [@liang2024mapping]. Comments from GPT-4 on research papers overlapped with human reviewers' about as much as two human reviewers overlapped with each other [@liang2024feedback]. Some go further: the AI Scientist writes, runs and reviews its own papers [@lu2024], and Agents4Science 2025 required AI authorship and used AI reviewers [@agents4science2025]. Writing with a model also changes the writer. A writing assistant set to argue one side shifted both what people wrote and what they later said they believed [@jakesch2023]. There is an older line of work on documents that carry their own computation, from literate programming [@knuth1984] to executable research articles [@maciocci2019]. Models and Words is built on that line: the journal is itself an agent, and each article is written with it, in a repository that holds the paper, its code and its review.

The difficulty is keeping the human in the loop. @mitchell2026 argue that current agent designs make oversight hard, and that relying on agents erodes the skills that oversight depends on. Among knowledge workers, higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking [@lee2025], and in a small study of essay writing, people who used a language model had the lowest sense of ownership of their essays and struggled to quote from them [@kosmyna2025]. @bucinca2021 showed that cognitive forcing, in which people commit to their own judgement before seeing the AI's, reduces overreliance, though people liked those designs least. Our teaching on doing statistics with an agent is built on the same idea. The agent does one step and stops, and the person makes each choice along the way, which requires enough grounding in the methods to judge what the agent proposes. The way articles are written for Models and Words follows this approach.
