## Abstract

Articles in Models and Words are about understanding social, biological, medical, sporting and technological systems, about the models we build of them, and about how those models relate to the words we use to describe them. We are looking for three types of contributions:

1. Applications which combine models and words to produce insight.
2. Methodological and theoretical contributions where models and words are combined.
3. Description of Agentic AI solutions, where human decision-making is placed in the centre.

Models and Words is itself an AI agent. Submissions are written together with an agent, reviews will be semi-automated and the repository itself can provide help and support to application builders and researchers.

## What we aim to publish

New models change how we see the world. Kermack and McKendrick's model of epidemics showed that an outbreak can end before everyone has been infected [@kermack1927]. Turing proposed that two chemicals reacting and diffusing could produce the spots and stripes on animals [@turing1952], and Vicsek and Couzin showed that animals following simple rules about their neighbours' positions and directions produce the ordered motion of flocks and schools [@vicsek1995; @couzin2002]. In social science, Schelling showed that people with only mild preferences about their neighbours can produce strongly segregated cities [@schelling1971], an idea that grew into agent-based simulations of whole economies [@farmer2009; @axtell2025]. In sport, models of which space a football team controls [@spearman2018] and of the expected value of a basketball possession [@cervone2016] changed how games are analysed. @gyllingberg2023 argued that this art of formulating new models has (until recently) been neglected in favour of analysing the ones already written down. With agentic AI now able to systematically tick off many of the checklist of properties existing models have, @kasirzadeh2026 is among several researchers arguing for a greater focus on opening up new mathematical approaches. Coding has also changed. By the end of 2024, an estimated 29% of the Python functions contributed to open-source projects in the United States were generated by AI [@daniotti2025].  Programmers who used AI more often tried new libraries and new combinations of them, which the authors read as a move into areas of programming new to them 

Possibly an even more profound change brought about by Agentic AI lies in how it produces a new relationship between words and models. Until recently, a model reached the people who used it through a statistician or data scientist, who knew which model answered which question, what it assumed and when it should not be trusted. Language models can now do part of that work themselves, but on benchmarks built from published expert analyses the best agents still complete only a quarter to a third of the tasks [@majumder2025; @chen2025], and they tend to choose simpler analyses than the experts did [@gu2024blade]. Many models of the social world are stated in words, with their assumptions left informal rather than written as equations or code [@guest2021; @robinaugh2021]. Language models can now carry such knowledge into new settings: GPT-4 predicted the results of 70 survey experiments about as well as human forecasters, including studies published after its training data [@ashokkumar2026], and language-model agents can state a hypothesis as a causal model and test it in simulation [@manning2024]. These results blur the lines between what can be said mathematically or statistically and what can be said in human language.

Model and Words publishes reports which recognise these changes and look to shape a future approach to modelling with agentic AI.

We publish three types of article. The first type of article applies models and words to give people insight to act on. In the past year, applications that join language and statistical models have started to appear. For example, multi-agent systems have provided suggestions of how existing drugs might be used for new treatments [@gottweis2026,@ghareeb2026]  and studies have shown how research tasks can be carried out by agents throughout biomedicine [@huang2026]. Social systems have their own examples. An AI mediator that wrote group statements from participants' own opinions helped people on opposing sides of political issues find common ground, and its statements were preferred to those of human mediators [@tessler2024]. Personalised, evidence-based conversations with a language model reduced belief in conspiracy theories by about a fifth, and the effect lasted at least two months [@costello2024]. A language model fine-tuned on more than 160 psychology experiments predicted the choices of new participants better than existing cognitive models [@binz2025]. In the second Model and Words report, a conversational analyst is developed which chooses which model answers a footballing question and explains what the answer means [@sumpter2026earpiece].  

The second kind of article is methodological or theoretical, focusing in particular on relating models and words. Broadly speaking, there are at least three ways of getting from a model to words: build a model that can be read directly [@rudin2019], narrate the output of an explanation method afterwards [@zytek2024], or reverse-engineer the computations inside a neural network into human concepts [@bereska2024]. We welcome all three approaches. The first article in this report series gives an example of the first way: showing how to turn a statistical model of the data into a rule-based description, deciding what is said and in what order, and a language model then writes only that description out as fluent text [@caut2025]. 

A third kind of article describes methods for building agentic AI systems that work with humans. @schluntz2024 distinguish workflows, in which language models and tools follow predefined code paths, from agents, in which the model directs its own process and use of tools. Built well, such systems help the people who make the decisions. With an agent that drew on treatment guidelines and earlier tumour-board cases, junior doctors came close to the treatment choices of senior specialists [@zoller2026]. A diagnostic agent that checked how consistent its own answers were passed the uncertain cases to clinicians and was almost always right on the rest [@zhang2026]. In the United Kingdom, language-model tools that summarise asylum interviews saved caseworkers time, while the decision stayed with them [@homeoffice2025]. Models and Words is built on that line: the report repository is itself an agent that can .

Language models already take part in writing and reviewing science. An estimated 6.5 to 16.9 percent of peer-review text at four AI conferences in 2023 and 2024 was substantially modified by a language model [@liang2024monitoring], and the share of model-modified text in published papers has risen steadily, mostly in computer science [@liang2024mapping]. Comments from GPT-4 on research papers overlapped with human reviewers' about as much as two human reviewers overlapped with each other [@liang2024feedback]. A difficulty in this approach is keeping the human in the loop. @mitchell2026 argue that current agent designs make oversight hard, and that relying on agents erodes the skills that oversight depends on. Among knowledge workers, higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking [@lee2025], and in a small study of essay writing, people who used a language model had the lowest sense of ownership of their essays and struggled to quote from them [@kosmyna2025].

## Writing your article with the agent

Each article is written in a shared repository, in one folder that holds the article, its references, its code and the material it rests on. The author works with an AI coding agent, and the article is a single Markdown file that both of them edit and that is built into the published page. Every change is committed, so the history of the article, including which sentences the agent wrote and which the author changed, can be read back.

Writing follows six phases: sketch, sketch the abstract, revise, write the start and end together, revise the abstract, and simplify and revise. In the first, the agent gathers the material the article will rest on, such as code, saved model outputs, earlier papers and notes, and records where each came from. In the second, author and agent agree the three points the article makes and put them into a working abstract. The abstract is then the template that each later section is checked against: the Introduction gives one paragraph of background to each point, the Methods and Results take them in the same order, and the Discussion answers them.

The agent is instructed to keep the author's judgement at the centre, following @mitchell2026. The author decides what the article says, and is asked to go first: to name the point of a paragraph before it is drafted, the papers they know before a search, and what they expected before a result is written up. The agent writes its drafts directly into the article, where the author edits them, and reads the author's edits before its next change. It works one step at a time and stops, so that each step can be read before the next begins.

The agent checks the writing as it goes. Every quoted model output is taken from its saved file, and the build checks it word for word. Every cited work is one the agent has opened, and when a paper cannot be opened the author is asked to download it. Every number comes from a saved output. When the author corrects how the agent writes or works, the correction is added, with the sentence that prompted it, to a set of writing rules that every later article follows. This article was written that way, and its history is in its repository.
