# Building models in the age of machine learning and AI

> This document gives some notes on what I think are some intresting research questions moving towards better model building.
## Abstract

In recent years the biggest advances in the use of mathematical and computational techniques have come from machine learning and AI. Yet these methods do not (in a straightforward manner) provide mechanistic understanding of the systems they model. A computer is trained to play chess or predict the weather, without us including information about the mechanisms involved. This poses a challenge to how applied mathematicians work and the traditional modelling cycle: of formulating a mechanism in the form of a model, fitting the model to known data and making testable predictions. Some researchers have even questioned whether such a modelling cycle is needed at all in the age of AI.

This research tackles this challenge head-on, by rethinking how we should design and build models. We do so by studying both a very practical example, football (soccer), and very abstract examples, complex system models. The aim of the practical work is to build a mechanistic model of football, which allows us to understand what might happen in the game over a 5 second window. The aim of the theoretical work is to study complex systems models, such as cellular automata, self-propelled particles and current reinforced oscillators, with an aim of identifying the emergent patterns they produce. Together, these two approaches feed into an overarching aim to develop a modern, pluralistic approach to model building. We will learn more about the modelling process and attempt to develop a new way of approaching models of complex systems that simultaneously improves our understanding of these systems and our ability to predict their behaviour.

## Background and Motivation

### Two Cultures of Statistical Modelling

In 2001, Breiman described two cultures in statistical modelling. The first culture assumes that data is generated by a specific *mechanism*, while the other concerns itself solely with *data*, and treats the mechanism as unknown (Breiman 2001). Data-driven approaches are now more important than mechanistic approaches in, for example, playing board games (Silver et al. 2018), computer vision (Guo et al. 2022), image generation (De et al. 2022) and short-term weather forecasting (Lim et al. 2021). It is more difficult to point to specific examples where mechanistic models have shown the same sort of recent breakthroughs, although it might be argued that progress on machine learning for molecular dynamics (Noé et al. 2020) and nuclear physics (Bedaque et al. 2021) is achieved through a combination of mechanism and data. Overall, however, AI underlies the current revolution in the utilisation of data, with mechanistic approaches struggling to keep up. Projects such as Google's Alpha Fold; Open AI's Dall-E text to image generator; Chat GPT and Meta's natural language models illustrate some of the advanced capabilities of these techniques.

### Limitations of Current AI Approaches

There remain questions about how far-reaching this data-driven revolution is. Last year, a workshop at Princeton identified an extensive misuse of AI methods in social sciences, potentially leading to a reproducibility crisis, similar to that in psychology (https://reproducible.cs.princeton.edu). Similar worries have arisen in applications in medicine (Vollmer et al. 2020; Christodoulou et al. 2019) and biology (Baker 2018). Writing in Lancet, Bachtiger et al. found there were many overconfident (and ultimately misleading) claims that AI can be used to solve many of the important challenges arising in response to Covid-19 (Bachtiger et al. 2020).

The identification of these limitations has led to an increasing number of calls to change the way which we develop and evaluate the performance of machine learning models (Burnell et al. 2023; Gundersen et al. 2022; Odouard et al. 2022; Birhane et al. 2021). These critiques point out the limitations of benchmark problems for machine learning: where a narrow definition of accuracy gives an impression of steady improvement in, for example, reproducing language, motion generation and vision tasks. This definition can hide the fact that these models are improving simply because the models themselves encode more and more data (Odouard et al. 2022) or because they rely on key (non-generalisable) properties of the training set (Recht et al. 2019). In a recent article in Science, Burnell et al. (2023) emphasised that we should be able to understand models and systems in a granular way: by breaking down how features of the problem space affect performance of the system.

### An Example: Football

*Figure 1: Comparing mechanistic and machine learning approaches to modelling goal probability. To illustrate the difference between mechanistic and data-driven models consider an example from football (soccer). Figure 1 shows a model based on the geometry of shooting (mechanism) (Morales et al. 2018; Sumpter 2017) and one that predicts goal probability by fitting machine learning method to all the available data (Anzer et al. 2021). The advantage of the former is that it is interpretable (Rudin 2019): it allows us to explain the model to, for example, players in terms of the parameters (e.g. how much of the goal they can see). The advantage of the latter is it tends to make more accurate predictions (e.g. it gives a more accurate measure of the quality of future chances). This tension between mechanistic and data driven approaches will underlie many of the challenges in practical modelling work.

Football is an example of a complex system, which lies at the border between systems which are amenable to data-driven techniques and those in need to be understood using mechanistic models. As in many questions on collective motion, of both animals and of humans (Reynolds 1987; Herbert-Read et al. 2011; Moussaïd et al. 2011; MacGregor et al. 2020; Kording et al. 2006), a key research question is how individual interactions produce collective dynamics. Football is fundamentally a game of anticipation (Loffing et al. 2017; Rosalie et al. 2014; Roca et al. 2012). By identifying the specific cues teammates and opponents follow, the aim is to identify how the patterns they produce allow the team to play better.

The state-of-the-art in football analytics has developed rapidly over the last decade. In 2017, when Sumpter published his book Soccermatics, which sketched many of the approaches currently adopted in modern football, there were very few mathematical modelling treatments of the game (Sumpter 2017). This has changed rapidly over the last six years, with both research projects and increased interest in using modelling and machine learning inside clubs (Spearman et al. 2017; Bornn et al. 2018; Fernández et al. 2019; Bransen et al. 2021; Rudd 2011). Football data now provides the positions and velocities of the players (automated tracking data), which is then synchronised with data describing actions, such as passes, tackles, dribbles etc. (for more details see Herold et al. 2019). In addition to this scientific literature, there are many methods implemented in practice but not fully investigated in a research setting. These activities have meant that much of the state of the art has been developed as a set of practices both within footballing organisations and in the public domain, that has not yet penetrated academic research. This gives a rich source of material for investigating the nature of modelling in an area rich in data and ideas. The granular approach, which Burnell et al. (2023) appealed for, is taking some form in the study of soccer.

### Complex Systems and Emergence

In parallel to applied modelling of football, we can pursue a research direction which emphasises complex systems modelling in a more abstract form. Here we build on a literature of models of complex systems to use them to compare data-driven and mechanistic approaches. There has been increasing interest in using machine learning methods for learning patterns produced by dynamical systems, in particular those which are chaotic (Tang et al. 2020). A useful approach is to apply some of these methods to the study three types of dynamical systems: cellular automata (CA) (Wolfram 1983), self-propelled particle models (Vicsek et al. 2012; Strömbom et al. 2015) and current reinforced oscillators (Ma et al. 2013; Strogatz 2000). These models are chosen because they produce patterns that are sufficiently complex that they can be studied on a scale greater than the local interactions that produce them.

The aim of this part is to identify complex emergent patterns. In some early work, Mitchell and colleagues looked at how rules in cellular automata might be evolved to produce particular output patterns. More recently, Mordvintsev and co-workers looked at learning rules for creating particular output shapes (Mordvintsev et al. 2020) and it has been shown that CA rules can be learnt by convolutional neural networks and to classify them (Hudcová et al. 2022).

### Critical Complexity and Pluralistic Modelling

![Fish locomotion modelling from multiple perspectives](Attachments/linneasfisk7.eps)

*Figure 2: Under the pluralistic approach, fish locomotion (for example) can be understood and modelled in many different ways. When modelling the collective motion of fish schools, self-propelled particle models can be used to capture and explain their motion (a). However, most fish use burst and glide movement, where they alternate between accelerated motion and powerless gliding (b). This type of motion can be understood from a hydrodynamical perspective (c), or a behavioural ecology perspective (d), as there are both energetic (c) and perceptual benefits (d) of this type of motion. When swimming in pairs, the fish influence each others burst and glide motion (e). Intermittent locomotion can also be understood and modelled through neurobiological perspective, where the activity in the brain affects the motion (f).*

The overarching aim is to create new approaches to modelling of complex systems in the age of machine learning. In a recent article, my colleagues and I critiqued how mathematical biology, in particular, has failed to respond to rapid developments in modern machine learning. We argued that out of the three modelling activities — (1) formulating models; (2) analysing models; and (3) fitting or comparing models to data — inherent to mathematical biology, many applied mathematicians currently focus too much on activity (2) at the cost of activity (1). Both practical and theoretical work should focus on activity (1). The former in a more practical way, the latter in a more abstract way.

In order to help achieve this aim, we can develop a clearer methodology for modelling based on integrative pluralism (Mitchell 2002, 2006). Football is a complex system, in the sense that the boundaries of what defines the current problem to be solved (scoring, getting the ball into the opponents half, taking a throw-in, devising a new tactical approach, motivating players...) is fluid. It is very different from the closed games such as chess, but it is still governed by a set of rules and structures, making it less complex than many other aspects of human society. We can use a definition of complexity proposed by Paul Cilliers (2002) and Alicia Juarrero (2000). They describe complex phenomena as unfinalizible and inexhaustible, which means that we can never capture any given biological or social system entirety with models (Cilliers 2016; Di Paolo et al. 2018). Complex systems are open-ended, which means there is no uncontested way of telling whether what we have included in a model is crucial or what we have omitted as irrelevant is indeed so. Models can, according to the critical complexity approach, be contradictory: we can accept two incompatible predictions as both describing the same system.

This complexity-based view suggests that theoreticians should avoid specialising in any one modelling approach and try to find the right set of models to understand a particular system in a given context. We should follow Smaldino's argument that "it is time to focus on better practices for hypothesis generation. We need training programmes in model building and critique, plus consortia-building and funding programmes to invent and test measurements that make models tractable. Better methods will help us get the right answers; models and measurements will ensure we ask the right questions" (Smaldino 2019).

The way forward, we believe, is to view mathematical modelling in a more open way, one that admits biology, society and even sports are complex. The approach involves creating lots of different models to build up a broader understanding of emergent phenomena. It is this approach that is integrative pluralism (Mitchell 2006). An approach which emphasises creating many different types of models (as is done in modelling fish locomotion in Figure 2). *Our approach will provide a new way of decomposing complex systems using mathematical models.*

## Research Ideas

### Idea 1: Decomposing Football

Modelling the game of football poses both applied and theoretical research challenges. The applied challenge is clear: can we find tactical nuances and ways of playing which improve a team's chance of winning? The theoretical research challenge goes deeper: to what degree can we capture a complex human activity within the framework of mathematical models and machine learning? We can address both of these challenges by developing:

**Value and Positioning Models**  
Build models which evaluate actions in terms of the probability that performing them leads to goal and in terms of likelihood of success of those actions.

**Movement Models**  
Build models which describe how players move during different match situations.

**Five Second Predictions**  
Show how the components of football can be broken down into independent models, to predict and explain movement over a 5 second window.

The novelty of this approach lies in the creation of a state of the art interpretable model of football. It will provide tools which, for example, break down the qualities of a particular pass between players: how much did it increase the team's chance of scoring? were there better alternatives? did the defending player fail to narrow down the angle of the pass properly? We can also break down approaches to specific situations: is a team choosing the best way to play out from the goalkeeper? how should throw-ins be taken and defended? And even develop tactics: we can use the results of our model to develop a tactical tool, which looks at whether formations and attacking movements succeed and how they can be countered.

### Idea 2: Complex Systems Models

We can work in the opposite direction of the very practical approach to football. While the football work attempts to integrate all available empirical knowledge using models, this extends the pluralistic component of modelling. The critique of mathematical modelling we make above is that it is currently too conservative — driven by what is analytically tractable, rather than by what models might show. Here we look at ways of creating very different types of models, which may (eventually) find applications without concerning ourselves with applications initially.

Candidate models for this work include: cellular automata; self-propelled particles and current reinforced oscillators. These models have been studied elsewhere, but the novelty here is an emphasis on creativity, over mathematical tractability. We can find particularly complex patterns emerging from these three systems. We can then compare mechanistic and machine learning approaches to 'understand' these models: can we classify patterns? Can we describe systems in terms of their emergent properties? What are the relative advantages/disadvantages of data-driven vs. mechanistic approaches?

### Idea 3: A Pluralistic Approach to Modelling

The football and complex systems approaches represent two extremes of modelling approaches. The goal is to draw the lessons learned in these approaches together into a coordinated framework. This will be a very different framework than the current modelling cycle — of theory, empirical testing and model refinement — that is usually proposed when teaching modelling (Sumpter 2012). The most ambitious aspect is to provide a framework for **how to build a model**: based on a synthesis of applications and theory. The pluralistic approach will encourage the modeller to see the system they are modelling from different perspectives (as in Figure 2); the football work provides a concrete example of an application pursued in this way; the complex systems work provides new views of the system in the form of novel models; and the integration ties these approaches together.

To achieve this ambitious goal we can pursue three aims:

**Pluralistic Approach**  
Find best practices for modelling complex systems using a pluralistic approach and explain them in ways that mathematical modellers can understand.

**Decomposing Systems**  
Develop a method based on model cards for decomposing empirical systems in a way that encourages multiple views, rather than a single framework.

**Model Library**  
Provide model libraries, which can be used to help modellers quickly try out new ideas.

## Methodology

### Football: Valuing Actions

The first step is to develop a machine learning tool for describing the value of actions. We use a reinforcement learning approach to evaluate passes, runs, dribbles, throw-ins etc. Specifically, each pass of the ball can be seen as a transition, which ultimately leads to a reward (positive if a team scores) or negative if a goal is conceded (Rudd 2011). We can think of $s \in S(a)$ as the state of play given a previous action and $a \in A(s)$ as a possible action (pass, dribble shot, ...) in that state. We then can update the threat (value) $V(s,a)$, i.e. the probability a goal is scored as actions are carried out, using a reinforcement learning scheme of a form similar to:

$$
V'(s,a) = V(s,a) +\overbrace{P(\text{goal}|s, a =\text{shot}) P(a =\text{shot}|s)}^{\text{Shot attempt (reward)}}
+  \overbrace{\sum_{a' \in A(s)} P(a'|s,a) \sum_{s' \in S(a')} P(s'|s,a')  V(s',a')}^{\text{Pass or other action made}}
$$

where $P(a'|s,a)$ is the probability of the next action and $P(s'|s,a')$ is the probability of a state resulting from that action. Since it is the players who make these decisions, these probabilities are taken from the data.

The challenge is defining a state space, $S$, for this framework given the dynamic nature of the game. We can investigate two parallel approaches. The first is to extract features from the data. We know the positions of the players and their velocities at the time a pass is played. Relevant features in $S$ are, for example, distance from goal; direction and distance of pass; number of players a ball 'takes out'; the speed of the attacking team players; the speed of defending team players; how much space the ball is played in to and so on. The second is to simply use the data itself to learn value, without specifying features, using deep learning. $s$ in this case is a vector describing the $x$ and $y$ positions of all the players and the ball.

We hypothesise that feature extraction will outperform a purely data-driven approach when presented with out-of-sample data (taken, for example, from another league or even future seasons of the league), both because the data set is of limited size (compared to chess or computer games for example) and doesn't in itself reveal what the players are trying to achieve. Specifically, the measurements we have of $P(a'|s,a)$ are skewed towards actions players think will work, making it difficult to use this data to understand why certain actions fail. As a result, we believe a feature-based method will be easier to interpret. For example, in one earlier study we were able to measure how much more valuable (in terms of probability of scoring) dribbles conducted at high speed were compared to lower speed dribbles.

We can further investigate various forms of stratified sampling. This is important because high value actions in football happen less frequently. Teams frequently pass the ball around in their own half waiting to make progress, and this skews the sample of actions. By sampling passes (for example) uniformly across the pitch we improve the fit of our models. Another way of stratifying the data is around particular situations. We can build separate models for throw-ins, counterattacks, established possessions, build-ups etc. We can also change the target variable based on shorter term goals. For example, the aim of build-up play is primarily to transport the ball to the final third, so achieving this goal becomes the measure of threat, $T(s,a)$.

Here the five second idea is central. Football has a very short memory, in the sense that what happened roughly 10 seconds earlier has very little influence on what happens 10 seconds later in the sense that the threat $T_{t+10}(s,a)$ is largely independent of $T_{t}(s,a)$ (although the time scale is also something we can look more closely at). So, instead of always focusing on long-term target variables, we can look at sub-targets — retaining ball from throw-in, moving the ball in to opponents half. Here interaction with coaching staff is essential. We can ask what a team is trying to achieve as a short term goal in a situation and use a model to evaluate what is the most successful strategy. We can then feedback and discuss the model predictions with coaching staff.

An important part is creating a model framework that allows us to compare the advantages and disadvantages of various models in terms both of explainability and model fit. Such systematic comparisons are common in weather forecasting (James et al. 2018), for example. We can do this for the first time for collective activity of humans. As discussed earlier, various models have been fit, primarily by practitioners at large football clubs. But design decisions are hidden, both because they aren't published and, even in published articles, the decisions of what choices were made is opaque. We can set up a clear framework, where models are fit based on different playing situations (counterattack, build-up etc.), different target values (goal, shot, ball to final third), different features (speed of movement, team compactness) and different forms of stratified sampling. This will allow rigorous comparison of models.

### Football: Positioning Models

![Layers of a passing model](Attachments/PassModel.png)

*Figure 4: Building up layers of a passing model.*

A novel part is breaking down actions in terms of the key components which determine if they will be successful or not. A pass between two players can fail for a variety of reasons: if it is poorly struck, it can be blocked by defenders, the receiver can be poorly positioned, the passer can be under pressure from behind... Our approach is to build up independent layers which determine the success of an action (i.e $P(s'|s,a')$ such that $s'$ is in the set of actions where a team retain the ball) as a function of various aspects of the positioning of players. One such approach is illustrated in Figure 4. We take the situation of the pass itself (shown at the top) and we then calculate pitch control (the probability a player can receive the ball), the effect of the nearest defender (in blocking the pass) and the difficulty of making a pass to the edges of the pitch. The shading indicates the probability of success of an action. The product of these three then give the overall probability of success of the pass.

The approach here is a combination of machine learnt models from the success of actions. For example, we can look at the relative speed and velocity of nearby players when a pass is made and fit machine learning models to predict success. From a mechanistic point of view, we can use footballing knowledge of how defending players narrowing down angles or show the direction of play to make parameterisable models. We can also improve pass success models in a similar way, looking at the pressure on the receiver of the ball from nearby players. We can further investigate experimental data on passing accuracy in situations where players are under different levels of pressure.

While we have concentrated on the value of passes in this description, similar methods can be used to assess the value of defensive movement (how well did a player narrow down an angle or 'show' the opposing player away from goal); how different are different types of dribbles and so on. Each action poses its own challenges, and our answer will be to build up layered, explainable models of passing the ball.

Combining a value model with the positional models, as is done in the bottom of Figure 4, gives an instantaneous model of the game. It tells us for any given situation, what the value of that situation is to the teams. At this stage, we can build our first playbook, and get feedback from coaching staff on its applicability.

Once we have a layered model of this type, we can compare it to a deep learning solution (Fernández et al. 2019) and to a graphical neural network solution (Stöckl et al. 2021). We can implement the state-of-the-art models of this type on our data and compare them both in terms of prediction reliability, how they work on other leagues and in terms of explainability. This will give insight for the theoretical work and help justify any claims we make about the performance of our model.

### Football: Player Movements

The preceding two sections give a snapshots of the game. They describe what a player does next. The next step is to build models of how the players move in response to these events. For example, for each ball-passing context and in each direction (forward-backward in direction of play, left-right sideward across the pitch) separately, the movement of each player $i$ is modelled as a weighted sum of vectors describing: the player's initial velocity $v_{i}$; velocities of nearest teammates $v_{i,T1}, v_{i,T2}, v_{i,T3}$ and team average $v_{i,TA}$; velocities of nearest opponents $v_{i,O1}, v_{i,O2}, v_{i,O3}$ and opponent average $v_{i,OA}$; the displacement of the ball due to the pass $\Delta_{i,p}$; the player's distance to the pass destination $\Delta_{i,b}$; distances to nearest teammates $\Delta_{i,T1}, \Delta_{i,T2}, \Delta_{i,T3}$ and team average $\Delta_{i,TA}$; distances to nearest opponents $\Delta_{i,O1}, \Delta_{i,O2}, \Delta_{i,O3}$; and opponent average distance $\Delta_{i,OA}$. The mathematical model for player $i$ is thus:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overbrace{\vec{x}_{i}(t + \delta t)}^{\text{prediction}} & = \overbrace{\vec{x}_{i}(t)}^{\text{initial position}} + \sum_{n \in \{1,2,3\}}  \overbrace{\alpha_{i, i, n}(\vec{v}_{i}(t))^{n} }^{\text{initial velocity}} \\
&   + \sum_{j \in \{1,2,3,A\}} \overbrace{\alpha_{i,Tj, n}(\vec{v}_{i,Tj}(t))^{n} }^{\text{team velocities}}
+ \overbrace{\alpha_{i,Oj,n} (\vec{v}_{i,Oj}(t))^n }^{\text{opponent velocities}}\\
& + \sum_{k\in \{1,2,3,A\}} \overbrace{\beta_{i,Tk, n}(\vec{\Delta}_{i,Tk}(t))^n }^{\text{team distances}} + \overbrace{\beta_{i,Ok, n}(\vec{\Delta}_{i,Ok}(t))^n }^{\text{opponent distances}} + \overbrace{\sum_{k\in \{p,b\}} \beta_{i, k, n}(\vec{\Delta}_{i,k}(t))^{n} }^{\text{ball distances}}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\alpha_{i,j,n}$ and $\beta_{i,k,n}$ are the dynamical coefficients of the model, $t$ is the moment of passing and $\delta t$ is the pass duration. The model includes second ($n=2$) and third order ($n=3$) terms for each variable to capture non-linear dynamics.

![Predicted trajectories example](Attachments/deviation_plot.png)

*Figure 5: Example of predicted trajectories for the yellow team. The uninterrupted line shows the actual trajectory, with the big dot as their end location. The dotted line and big square shows respectively the predicted trajectory and end location*

We can fit the above equation to the data using regression for each positional role, independently. Note the equation includes the term $\vec{x}_{i}(0) + \alpha_{i, i, 1}\vec{v}_{i}(t)$, which is dead reckoning (e.g. continuing in the same direction) provided by the coefficient. We are thus investigating how our regression model improves on this baseline. Figure 5 shows examples of some predicted trajectories from a model of this type. The challenge is to identify the rules of motion of the players, which depend also on playing position and the tactical aims of the teams in these situations.

### Football: Five Second Model

The above three components can be combined into a five-second predictive model of football. This model will give, for any given situation, the probability of different outcomes in the future. It will also — because of the way we have built it on mechanisms — tell us why certain events are more likely to happen and be able to assign values to those actions. Specifically, we use the combined value and positioning model to identify potential actions. We then use the movement model to look at how the players will move in response to each action, simulating forward in time.

A key question here is how reliable a model of this nature is in predicting the flow of the game into the future? One way of measuring this is to look how the log-likelihood of the model predictions evolves as time goes on. We expect that at around 10 seconds or so, predictions will differ little from a sequence of 'random' actions. Another useful metric here is how many moves ahead a model can actually be useful. At some point the model will fail and produce unrealistic sequences of play. How far into future can we really predict football?

### Complex Systems: Cellular Automata

Cellular automata are a key example of models with simple rules that produce complex patterns (Wolfram 1984). For example, rule 110, of the elementary cellular automata is shown in Figure 3a. The pattern shows evolution over time, with each line from top to bottom resulting from the application of the rules from the row above. The 256 rules of elementary cellular automata (where interactions are between $n=1$ nearest neighbouring cells) have been categorised into four classes (stable, periodic, chaotic and complex) of which the rule shown is one of the few for which is complex. For $n=2$ there are $2^{32}$ possible rules, many of which are complex (Wolfram 2002).

An open research question here is automatically identifying rules which generate complex patterns. One early suggestion was that rules with high entropy would be more complex, but Mitchell and co-workers showed this was not the case. Some preliminary work suggests that machine learning methods can be used to find complex rules. Specifically, if we learn the rules on local observations on the simulations output of rows (after a transient phase) then those with more complex rules are *easier* to learn using a neural network. This result occurs because the long-term dynamics of complex models visit more of the state space than periodic or stable models. On the other hand, it also appears that on a larger spatial scale, in terms of the whole or large parts of the emergent pattern (in Figure 3a for example) is more *difficult* to learn, in the sense that an auto-encoded version of output samples is higher dimensional (Wang et al. 2016). This provides us with a testable hypothesis about these systems — they are easy to learn on the scale corresponding to the rules but difficult to learn on the level of emergent patterns — which is consistent with the philosophy of complex systems — simple (easy to learn) local rules produce complex (difficult to categorise) patterns. We can pursue this and other ways in automatically identifying complex emergent structures.

Better understanding of complexity in CAs (and the other two study systems, see below) will provide us with a set of models with simple underlying rules or equations, that produce very complicated emergent dynamics. In turn, these will provide a test bed for comparing mechanistic and data-driven methods (Breiman 2001). For example, one part of the data-driven approach is explainable machine learning (Roscher et al. 2020) where visualization of the layers of the network are used to gain insight into the features the network is using to learn. This has been used, for example, to explain to doctors what features are key and to identify where spurious artefacts are used to make predictions. The advantage of our models is that we know the causal rules which produce the output, because we created them. We can thus produce a more thorough investigation of what can and can't be concluded about a system using explainability methods. For example, in recent studies of language models, these methods are used to identify structures inside the learnt model corresponding to grammatical constructions (Clark et al. 2019). But how reliable are such visual methods for reaching conclusions about what the network has learnt remains unclear. Because we have access to the inner workings of the model, we can systematically investigate whether we infer correct information about the underlying system when we create such visualisations.

The value of this work lies in first finding a range of complex models and then using them as test beds for thinking about how we do modelling in data-driven vs mechanistic approaches. *We expect to identify a set of models that can be well-explained by a mechanistic approach but are inaccurately represented by a purely data-driven approach.*

### Complex Systems: Current Reinforced Oscillators

The procedure adopted for CAs can also be adopted to other models. We have recently designed a new modelling approach based on coupling between two previous models — coupled oscillators (Strogatz 2000) and reinforced random walks (Ma et al. 2013). This model is inspired by acellular slime mould, *Physarum polycephalum*, which shows various oscillatory phenomena (Boussard et al. 2019; Smith et al. 1992; Kobayashi et al. 2006; Kauffman 1975; Rodiek et al. 2015) and remarkable problem solving skills (Dussutour et al. 2021; Reid et al. 2016; Beekman et al. 2015). A link between oscillations and problems solving skills in slime moulds has not been demonstrated, but Boussard et al. (2021) hypothesize that modulation of spontaneous oscillations may sustain learning in slime mould. 

To examine this theoretically, we formulated the following differential equation model of how the nodes ($N_i$) which make up a slime mould's body connect:

$$
\frac{dN_i(t)}{dt} = a_i(t) - b N_i(t) + \sum_{j\in\Gamma(i)}\frac{N_j(t) - N_i(t)}{l_{ij}}D_{ij}(t),
$$

where $\Gamma(i)$ are the neighbours of $i$, with the conductivity of each edge $(v_i,v_j)\in E$ changing as the following:

$$
\frac{dD_{ij}(t)}{dt} = q\frac{|N_i(t) - N_j(t)|}{l_{ij}}D_{ij}(t) - \lambda D_{ij}(t),
$$

which captures current reinforcement. We set

$$
a_i(t) = A(1 + \sin(2\pi\theta t + \phi_i(t)))
$$

to be an oscillator, which has a phase, $\phi_i(t)$ that changes as a function of $N_i$ and its neighbours $N_i \in \Gamma(i)$. This model can generate a whole range of dynamics, including efficient transport networks (Figure 3b), localised oscillatory behaviour and global oscillatory dynamics. Again, like CAs, the key interesting feature is the complex global patterns produced. We have already found intriguingly complex dynamics in small networks of these models. Further investigation, and comparison to slime mould behaviour, should help guide us towards identifying complex emergent patterns in this system.

### Complex Systems: Self-Propelled Particles

Self-propelled particles (SPPs) are another well studied type of complex systems modelling, both in terms of applications (Huth et al. 1992; Tunstrøm et al. 2013; Romenskyy et al. 2017; Strömbom et al. 2014; Nagy et al. 2010) and in terms of theoretical results (Vicsek et al. 1995; Czirók et al. 1999; Grégoire et al. 2003, 2004; Vicsek et al. 2012; Grossmann et al. 2014). There remains (surprisingly) a gap in the literature when it comes to investigating the range of potential emergent patterns in these models. Figure 3c shows one study which identified eight model phases from a model based on a blind angle and attraction alone (Strömbom et al. 2015). We can use auto-encoders and other machine learning methods to identify parameters that produce complex dynamics.

The self-propelled particle approach will allow us to relate the abstract modelling back to the football work, which is also focused on creating an SPP model, but in that case from data. Can we find ways to produce complex fluid movement of sports teams without starting from data? Could such models then be related back to our empirical observations in the football work?

### Integrative Pluralism: Best Practices

Once both practical and theoretical modelling work is underway, we can begin studying how researchers work and how to build a framework of best practices around modelling. Here we build on three pieces of previous work:

1. Sandra Mitchell emphasises that multilevel, multi-component, complex systems cannot be reduced to a simple, unified picture of scientific theorizing (Mitchell 2003). And even though contributions to mathematical biology can be made by models, they do not account for all the explanations that biologists seek (Mitchell 2006). Similar ideas, applied to economics and social sciences, have been presented by others (Knuuttila 2011; Rodrik 2015).

2. Andrew Gelman's work on the best practice in statistical methods (Gelman et al. 2017) (see also the replies to Breiman 2001). Gelman and Hennig argue that in statistics, any notion of objectivity should be "replaced by transparency, consensus, impartiality and correspondence to observable reality, while subjectivity replaced by awareness of multiple perspectives and context dependent" (Gelman et al. 2017).

3. Margaret Mitchell and colleagues have produced an approach to documentation based on, what they call, *model cards for model reporting* (Mitchell et al. 2019). They describe model details; intended use; demographic and environmental factors; metrics used to measure performance; evaluation data; training data; qualitative analyses; ethical considerations; and caveats. This method has since been incorporated at Hugging Face (https://huggingface.co), where the most frequently downloaded models are more thoroughly documented using model cards.

Our research is about how we can combine these three views in both a conceptual and a technical framework for best modelling practice. While mathematical modellers are very good at developing new analytical methods and mathematical theory, we tend to be less concerned with a theory of how we go about our work when we apply models to the real world. It is this gap which we intend to address.

The key to the pluralistic approach is the adoption of multiple views of a system (as illustrated in Figure 2). This work will look at how we can shape the dialogue around mathematical modelling, which tends to be mainly reductionist (Dupré 1983). Modellers often use the statement by George Box — 'All models are wrong, but some are useful' — to remind us not confuse models with reality. Yet, often only one model is presented per scientific article. This work will challenge how we approach modelling and look for alternative best practices.

We can look into ways to encourage a culture within modelling which focuses on building different models of systems and integrating that knowledge. Some examples of useful advice, following Sandra Mitchell, might be encouraging modellers to report what their model doesn't explain, as well as what it does explain. To actively challenge modellers to take more than one approach to any problem. To think about how models allow us to make analogies across systems. Following Andrew Gelman, we might also create a list properties, such as, transparency, consensus, awareness of context dependence, and so on which to base modelling approaches (Gelman et al. 2017). We can look at each of these properties in the context of both practical and theoretical work, as well as other applications.

A first step towards this goal has been made by Veit (2020). He extends the idea of integrative pluralism to what he calls "model pluralism" and highlights two core theses for modelling: (1) any successful analysis of models must target sets of models, their multiplicity of functions within science, and their scientific context and history and (2) for almost any aspect $x$ of phenomenon $y$, scientists require multiple models to achieve scientific goal $z$. Our aim (outlined below) to build model cards for system understanding and model library will be a concrete example of thesis (1).

The football and complex systems work proceed in two different directions towards integrative pluralism. The football work takes a very practical example of an application and the complex systems work takes a very theoretical view. The fact they represent two extremes allows us to work on a full range of modelling approaches. Moreover, our specific choice for these two areas arises from a hypothesis that these extremes are likely to provide the biggest insights into how the best models are built: by getting into details (as we do in football) we are forced to make modelling decisions grounded in practice and by building models that express complexity, we have the best chance of creating new lenses with which to view the world.

### Integrative Pluralism: Decomposing Systems

![Model cards system](Attachments/ModelCards.png)

*Figure 6: For each system (on the left) we create a series of model cards explaining how we have used models to understand it. The model library provides different techniques which can be used to aid understanding.*

One issue with current mathematical modelling approaches is that models are not always reproducible (Schnell et al. 2018). Although defined in the text of a paper with equations, it is not always straightforward to use these descriptions to reproduce the results. On the other hand, sometimes mathematical frameworks are proposed for understanding a system (Cudmore et al. 2021) based on one specific formalism. Frameworks are not ideal either, because under a pluralistic approach we strive for variety of modelling approaches. We need to find a way of decomposing systems in different ways, which allows us to preserve the flexibility plurality, while allowing us to work in an open science framework that allows reproducibility of results.

This work will focus on how we decompose systems using models (Burnell et al. 2023). We can look into ways of curating and documenting models of systems that allow for multiple approaches, following the pluralistic approach. The methodology will build on the model cards idea, but with an emphasis on *system understanding*. For any particular system, we can create a series of model cards which describe the way we have modelled it (illustrated on the left of Figure 6). Rather than emphasising a single formalism our model cards approach will emphasise the assumptions we made in the model, the aspect of the system we were trying to capture, the relationship between models and the insights gained from the model. The research is about how these cards should be designed. We can work both with researchers in various fields to develop a system which can be used to describe a pluralistic approach to modelling a system, which can work across research groups.

Another aspect to consider here is how we can integrate our model cards for system understanding with the trustworthy AI framework the EU have adopted (Floridi et al. 2019). Although the models proposed here might not fall under the usual usage of the term AI, we are developing approaches to capture system behaviour in both a concrete and abstract sense. The method encourages transparency and accountability, but we can also look at how this modelling approach incorporates privacy and data governance, societal well-being and diversity and non-discrimination. For example, how do models in football differ between men and women, and what are the consequences for their usage.

This approach will also help us more systematically compare between data-driven and mechanistic methods. One model card can describe a purely data-driven attempt to understand a system, describing the input data, the output data and the machine learning model used. Another model card might describe insight and how a model changed our view of a system. It can document the insight and feedback it provided and the degree to which results could be interpreted.

### Integrative Pluralism: Model Library

One of the greatest success stories in machine learning are libraries — such as ScikitLearn, TensorFlow and PyTorch — for working in machine learning. In applied mathematics, the comparable tools might be considered to be libraries for solving differential equations. But what is missing is a ready prepared library of useful models. While simpler models (like logistic growth or predator prey models) are relatively easy to implement, more complex models (like those described above) are not part of a model package. There are examples of webpages with interactive demos of models — for example, Stefan Bornholdt's interactive models (http://www.itp.uni-bremen.de/complex/interactive-models/) and Santa Fe's Complexity explorer (https://www.complexityexplorer.org) — but these do not provide easily reusable code and Github libraries.

The model library (illustrated on the right of Figure 6) differs from the model card approach in that the focus is on documenting what models help us explain. This dovetails with the complex systems work, where we develop new models. The idea here is to provide mathematical models with a rich tapestry of complex behaviour which can be used (but also simply inspire) those modelling on applications. We can find consistent ways of documenting models, not just in terms of their equations but also in terms of the patterns they can produce.

## Concluding Thoughts

The approach outlined here represents a new way of thinking about mathematical modelling in the age of AI. Rather than seeing mechanistic and data-driven approaches as competing, we can explore how they complement each other. Through the concrete example of football, we ground our ideas in practical application. Through abstract complex systems models, we push the boundaries of what models can express. And through the pluralistic framework, we develop a new culture of modelling that embraces multiple perspectives.

The key insight is that complexity demands plurality. No single model captures a complex system. Instead, we need a constellation of models, each illuminating different aspects, each contributing to a richer understanding. This is not a weakness of modelling but its strength — when we embrace it fully and develop the tools and practices to work this way effectively.

The path forward involves creating models, not just analysing them. It involves transparency about assumptions and limitations. It involves building tools and libraries that make pluralistic modelling practical. And ultimately, it involves changing the culture of mathematical modelling to embrace the creative, exploratory, and inherently plural nature of understanding complex systems.
