### Wordalisation test (Functions › Structured wordalisations)

Agreed with David, 18 October 2026: the same questions put to Earpiece and to agents with Twelve's raw data, judged blind by a further Claude on the criteria of Caut et al. (2025).

- Players (`Code/wt_players.py`, seed 18): 10 women and 10 men from the name-test sample, outfield, at least 900 minutes, spread over positions.
- Questions (`Code/wt_answers.py`): ten that Earpiece's Scout is designed for, from its instructions and its functions' descriptions: season summary; strengths and weaknesses; passing; dribbling; threat in the box; where on the pitch the player wins the ball back; another position; pressing; in the air; involvement. Transfer, team fit and comparison are left out because they use prediction models the agents cannot reach. A pilot on two players replaced three questions: "Where on the pitch is {name} most effective?" names no quality, which the pitch function needs, and two general questions ("Can the opposition get past {name}?", "Does {name} make poor decisions?") returned Earpiece's error message.
- Systems, three repeats each: Earpiece's Scout, opened in the player's context as the app does; Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-4o (Azure), each with the 35 raw data tools of Twelve's MCP server (no Earpiece description tools, no knowledge tools), run in-process on the same data functions, given the player's context and the one-line system prompt "You are a football analyst. Answer using the tools. Answer in exactly {n} sentences, in plain prose.", n being the number of sentences in Earpiece's answer to the same question, player and repeat (David: "Make sure Claude is asked to answer in same number of sentences as the Earpiece answer"). The pilot's first wording, "Answer in {n} sentences", was exceeded often and was tightened.
- Judge (`Code/wt_judge.py`): Claude Opus 5.5, one answer per call, not told which system wrote it, markdown bold and italics removed. Criteria from Caut et al. (2025, section 4.2): faithfulness to the information, engagement, usefulness for understanding or decision-making, each 1–5 (their paper gives no scale), and hallucinations ("unsupported or fabricated claims") listed. Three independent levels of information: (1) the data either system could draw on: the player's quality and metric z-scores, the zone z-scores Earpiece's pitch descriptions use, and a fixed set of the agents' data-tool calls (biography, career by position, season metrics, defensive metrics, metrics by zone) (`Code/wt_rawdata.py`); (2) level 1 plus the question–answer pairs explaining the data in the prompt of the Earpiece function(s) that answered ("tell it what it knows"); (3) level 2 plus Earpiece's synthetic text. Levels 2 and 3 are taken from Earpiece's first repeat, so all answers to a question about a player are judged against the same information. Pilot changes: the player's full name added (Earpiece's pitch texts use it), and level 1 widened from season values alone, against which both systems' zone and career statements were judged invented.
- Summary (`Code/wt_summary.py`): means with 95% intervals from a bootstrap over players, the share of cases in which each system scores highest, by question and by gender, with and without Earpiece's error or empty answers.
- Run 18 October 2026: 600 answers per system (Earpiece API `d2077f55`); 5,394 judgements, every non-empty answer at all three levels (two Earpiece answers were empty, so 6 judgements fewer than 5,400). Earpiece gave its error message in 25 answers, all to question 10, whose answer went to the general question function in 25 of 60 cases, and nothing in 2 more. The agents kept to Earpiece's sentence count within one sentence in most answers; the Claude agent's answers average 4.1 sentences and 770 characters against Earpiece's 3.6 and 586, GPT-4o's 3.6 and 474. The judge's token limit was raised from 1,500 to 3,000 for the last two calls, whose earlier replies had held no verdict; where an answer was judged twice the first judgement counts. Results in `outputs/wordalisation-test/summary.json`.
- Second judgement (David, 19 October 2026), kept beside the first: the same answers, levels and judge, with engagement and usefulness defined in David's words: "an engaging text is one which tells the scout about the player in footballing terms without overwhelming with numbers and stats. If it provides a number it should do so in context of comparing to other players. A text is only useful if it compares a player to other similar players. If it provides numbers it should explain what they mean." Faithfulness and hallucinations as before. Prompted by David's reading of the Claude agent's answers as dense lists of numbers, rated engaging and useful by the first judge. `Code/wt_judge.py --variant defined`; results in `judgments-defined.jsonl`, `summary-defined.json`, `judgments-defined.csv`. Runs on the workspace's own Anthropic key.
- How the second judgement was run (David chose, 19 October 2026): not on an API key but by ten Claude Code subagents on David's Claude account, on a sample: 10 players (5 women, 5 men, seed 19), all ten questions, repeat 1, all three systems, 300 answers, at level 3 (`Code/wt_agent_batches.py`). One subagent per player read the player's data and each question's explanations and synthetic text once, then rated the 30 answers, shuffled and under opaque ids, one by one. These ratings are not independent calls as in the first judgement. Collated and compared with the first judgement's level-3 ratings of the same 300 answers by `Code/wt_agent_collect.py` (`judgments-defined-agents.jsonl`, `summary-defined-agents.json`), with the number of figures per sentence counted by script.
- Full second judgement (David, 19 October 2026: "run the full test"): all 1,800 answers at all three levels, 5,394 judgements, the same definitions and batch method, by 60 subagents, one per player and level, each rating up to 90 answers (10 questions × 3 repeats × 3 systems) shuffled under opaque ids (`Code/wt_agent_batches.py --full`). All 5,394 rated, none missing.

### Name test (Contexts and names)

Agreed with David, 17 October 2026: 400 players (300 men, 100 women) from less-known leagues, each asked about with a misspelled name and, as a control, correctly spelled, to see whether the receptionist's redirect reaches the right player.

- Sample (`Code/name_test_sample.py`): from Earpiece's own data, so each player has the id a correct resolution must reach. Domestic adult leagues in their latest season (2025 or 2026), at least 450 minutes, leaving out the men's Big Five and the women's top divisions of England, the United States, Spain, Germany and France; competitions shuffled (seed 2026), up to three players each. Drawn: 300 men from 101 competitions, 100 women from 35. Transfermarkt was considered as the source of names and set aside: its players would first have to be matched to the data's ids, and its terms forbid scraping.
- Error model (`Code/name_test_misspell.py`, seed 1992), from the literature, which David asked for: Christen (2006, TR-CS-06-02, section 2), reporting Friedman and Sideli (1992, *Computers and Biomedical Research* 25:486–509, not opened) on patient names: 36% of errors an added or missing name word, initial or title; 39% single errors; 14% several letters changed by spelling variation; 12% punctuation and whitespace. Udupa and Kumar (2010, EMNLP, pages 1256–1265): phonetic and cognitive errors in names; misspellings from a real people-search log lay 2.33 edits (SD 1.60) from the name, against 1.39 for a list of common misspellings. Shares 36:39:14:12 scaled to 400; within single errors substitution > deletion > insertion > transposition (Christen's order; the 4:3:2:1 weights are ours); keyboard-neighbour substitutions; lower-casing counted under punctuation, as in Earpiece's own test queries. A quarter of names hard: letter-level errors first until three letter edits (two for names under ten letters), then any name-word change. Result: 158 single typing errors, 139 name words, 53 spelling variants, 50 punctuation, spacing and accents; mean letter edits 0.67 (normal) and 3.06 (hard).
- Query: "How has {name} played this season?", to the receptionist as a new chat, once per player and condition; outcome right, asked the user, wrong player or other (`Code/name_test_run.py`). Rates with 95% Wilson intervals by condition, gender, category, normal or hard, and edit distance (`Code/name_test_summary.py`).
- Run 17 October 2026, Earpiece API `d2077f55`, 800 runs (receptionist on GPT-4o, disambiguation on GPT-4o-mini); results in `outputs/name-test/summary.json`.
- Second run, with teams (David, 17 October 2026: "rerun the same players but this time with the teams, also with spelling errors or nicknames in the error case"). Question "How has {name} played for {team} this season?"; correct case: the data's player and team names; error case: the same misspelled player name and an informal or misspelled team name (`Code/name_test_teams.py`, seed 17). Informal name: the club's name without generic parts (FC, IF, Club …), or for a name of several words its longest word ("Holstein Kiel" → "Holstein"); half the teams, but the data's names are mostly short already, so one-word names got a misspelling instead: 123 informal, 274 misspelled. No source was found for clubs' real nicknames, so none were used. Three players whose team has no name in the data are left out: 397 players, 794 runs. Results in `outputs/name-test/runs-team.jsonl` and `summary-team.json`.
